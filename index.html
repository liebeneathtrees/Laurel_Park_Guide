Quick start

  • Tap any pin, or a name under Species, to open its field notes.
  • Tap the arrow button to see where you are. What's near me? (top of the species list) shows the closest species.
  • Open Learn to tick off species in Challenge Yourself, or Take the Quiz.
  • Long-press the map, or tap the camera button, to save your own pin or photo.
  • Tap Share on any card to send a link straight to it.
📱Getting around on a phone
  • Five tabs along the bottom: Map, Species, Learn, My Pins, and More (About, Trails, By the Numbers, contact details).
  • Each tab slides up as a sheet over the map. To close it: tap the same tab again, tap Map, tap ✕, or swipe it down.
  • A species card opens at half height so the map stays visible. Swipe up for full screen; swipe down to shrink or close it.
  • On a computer, everything is in the menu on the left instead.
🗺️Using the map
  • Pan by dragging; zoom with + and − or a pinch.
  • Layers icon (stacked squares, top right): switch between Satellite, OpenTopoMap, USGS Topo, and Street map, and turn Trails and Areas on or off.
  • Funnel icon (next to it): choose which pins show, by Type, Habitat, and Native status, plus Community Sightings and Community Picks.
  • Bottom right: the camera button (snap a photo pin), the location button, and zoom.
  • Zoomed out, nearby pins group into a numbered circle. Zoom in, or tap it, to spread them out.
  • Teal-ringed pins are Community Picks; plain pins, where a guide has them, are species verified in person.
🌿Species cards
  • Every species is in one A-Z list under Species, grouped by Trees, Plants & Wildflowers, Animals, and so on.
  • Each card has tabs: Overview, Ecology, Uses (edible or medicinal, where relevant), Caution (only when there's a real hazard), and Connections.
  • Badges show its habitat and whether it's native, introduced, or invasive; the Role chip sums up its job in the ecosystem.
  • I found this one adds it to your Challenge list.
🥾Challenge Yourself
  • Under Learn on phones. Tap a species' circle when you find it; your list stays on this device.
  • Starter 10 is a hand-picked set that's easy to recognize; In season now matches the current month; All covers every species.
  • Earn badges for your first find, halfway, and completing a list. With your location on, it also points you to the nearest one you haven't found.
  • Share my progress makes an image of your list to share or save; Reset my list starts over.
❓Take the Quiz
  • Under Learn on phones. Five levels: Seedling (nature basics), Sprout (identifying trees, birds, tracks, and look-alikes), Explorer (how ecosystems work), Naturalist (conservation and what you can do), and Expert (names, conservation history, and deeper science).
  • Each level has about 30 questions, every one checked against a trusted source, and each round deals 10. Score 7 out of 10 to unlock the next level. Your best score on each level is kept on this device.
  • Every answer shows a short explanation and a Learn more link to its source.
  • At the end you'll see your score and a review of any questions you missed, with the right answers.
🔗Sharing
  • Share on a card opens your phone's share sheet (or copies the link on a computer). The link opens straight to that species.
  • The address bar always matches the open card, so you can copy a link from there too.
⭐Community Picks
  • Real, iNaturalist-documented species written up for this guide, marked with a teal ring on the map.
  • Each card credits the observer and links to the original observation.
  • A pin shows where the species was documented, not a guarantee it's still there today. Some observers' apps didn't record how precise a location is; those cards say so.
🕸️How It All Connects
  • Under Species on phones: every real ecological link between two species in the guide, such as who eats, pollinates, or shelters whom.
  • Tap either name to jump to its card.
📊Laurel Park by the Numbers
  • Under More on phones: how many observations and species naturalists have recorded here on iNaturalist, the busiest months, and the most-recorded species.
  • A real sample of what's been documented, refreshed from time to time, not a full census.
🛤️Trails & areas
  • Trails appear as dashed lines and are listed under Trails (More on phones) with their length: tap one to find it on the map.
  • Shaded areas, where a guide has them, mark a specific place such as a meadow or habitat zone.
  • Turn either on or off from the layers icon.
👀Community sightings
  • An optional layer of raw iNaturalist observations near the park, separate from the Community Picks.
  • Turn it on in the funnel panel's Show section; extra checkboxes then let you show just birds, plants, insects, and so on.
  • Tap a dot for the photo, date, and a link to the observation.
📍Finding your location
  • The location button (navigation arrow) shows you as a blue dot and follows you as you walk.
  • On phones with a compass, a blue arrow on the dot shows which way you're facing (iPhones ask permission the first time).
  • What's near me? at the top of the species list shows the 15 closest pins with distance and direction, updating as you walk, with a check on ones you've found.
  • Location needs your permission, only works on the live website, and is never sent anywhere.
🛰️If your location seems off
  • Phones often start with a rough estimate from cell towers and Wi-Fi while GPS locks in. That can take a few seconds to a couple of minutes, longer under trees.
  • A message shows Finding GPS... with the accuracy so far, then GPS locked in. The map waits for a good fix before moving.
  • Standing in the open helps, and so, sometimes, does briefly opening another map app.
  • Other map apps can be exact while this guide is rough, because a web page gets location through your browser's permission. Make sure your browser is allowed precise location, then reload:
    Safari on iPhone: Settings, Privacy & Security, Location Services, Safari Websites, Precise Location.
    Chrome on iPhone: Settings, Chrome, Location, Precise Location.
    Android: Settings, Location, App location permissions, Chrome, Use precise location.
  • Opened from a Home Screen icon? Also try it in the browser, or the other way around; they can have separate settings.
  • To see exactly what your phone reports, add #gps-check to the end of the guide's web address. Copy report sends the details to the guide's maintainer.
📌Saving your own pins and photos
  • Long-press the map (right-click on a computer) to drop a pin. Choose where you pressed or your GPS location, pick Plant, Animal, or Not Sure, and add a name, notes, and a photo if you like.
  • Or tap the camera button to take the photo first: the form opens with it attached and your GPS location selected.
  • Pins show as solid red circles and stay on this device. Tap a thumbnail to see the photo full screen.
  • Send (or Download for email) shares a pin with the guide's maintainer; on phones the note and photo go out together.
  • Export / Upload My Data saves everything, photos included, to a file you keep and can load on another device.
  • On iPhone, add the guide to your Home Screen (Share, then Add to Home Screen): Safari can clear saved data for sites you haven't visited in a while, but not for Home Screen guides.
📅This month at the park
  • The highlighted box lists what's flowering, fruiting, or otherwise in season right now, based on your device's clock.
  • Tap an entry to jump to that species. It's near the top of the menu on a computer, and under Species (below the list) on phones.
📶Using the guide offline

Cell signal can drop out along the shore, so this guide is built to work without a connection. Open it once while you have signal, then check Use Offline (under More on phones): it says "Ready" once the guide is saved on your device. After that, every species card, the Trail Challenge, the quiz, and your pins and photos work offline, and a banner tells you when you've lost signal.

Map tiles you've already looked at are kept. To be sure an area is there later, zoom to it while connected and tap Save this map view: it shows about how many tiles and megabytes that takes before saving. Remove saved tiles frees the space again, and Add to home screen keeps the guide like an app. The location dot and "What's near me?" use your phone's GPS, which usually keeps working without cell signal. Community Sightings show the last set that loaded, and sharing or sending pins waits until you have signal again.

✉️Questions, comments, or suggestions
  • The contact section (bottom of the menu, or under More on phones) has an email link for general feedback.
  • To suggest a plant or animal for the guide, save a personal pin and use its Send or Download option, so the location, notes, and photo come with it.

An interactive record of the trees, plants, and animals found along the Lake Lanier shore at Laurel Park, Gainesville, GA.

About & Visiting Info

Laurel Park is a 124-acre Hall County park on the shore of Lake Lanier, north of Gainesville. Alongside its ballfields, splash pad, playgrounds, and dog parks, a paved walking trail runs through the wooded shoreline under mature hardwoods. The park's Mega Boat Ramp, built by Hall County with the Georgia Department of Natural Resources through the Go Fish program, reaches about 350 feet out so the lake stays reachable when the water is low.

Native vs. Invasive at This Park

Like much of the Southeast, the land around Lake Lanier carries the fingerprints of centuries of human movement. Species introduced from Asia and Europe, often planted intentionally for ornamental beauty or erosion control, have spread well beyond where they were planted, and now compete with native species across Georgia. As species get added to this guide, look for the status badge on each card to see which is which.

Even a species with some redeeming value, like feeding pollinators or birds, can still cause harm when it spreads unchecked: a landscape dominated by a few aggressive newcomers supports less overall biodiversity than one built on a full community of native species.

Native Introduced Invasive

Look for this status badge on each species card, and a small warning dot on invasive species' map pins. Where relevant, we've noted a native alternative that fills a similar role. You can help track the spread of invasive species through Georgia's Early Detection & Distribution Mapping System (EDDMapS).
